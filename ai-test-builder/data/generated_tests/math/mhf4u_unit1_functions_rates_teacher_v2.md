**1. Piecewise functions [9]**  
a. Draw the line `y=x+3` from filled `(-4,-1)` toward open `(0,3)`; draw `y=3-x^2` from filled `(0,3)` through `(1,2)` to filled `(2,-1)`; draw `y=x-1` for `2<x<=4`, with open `(2,1)` and filled `(4,3)`. [4]  
b. Domain `[-4,4]`; range `[-1,3]`. [2]  
c. The first branch increases from `x=-4` to `x=0`, and the third branch increases for `2<x<=4`. Accept `[-4,0]` and `(2,4]` as branch intervals. [1]  
d. No. A horizontal line such as `y=2` meets the graph at more than one point: `P(-1)=2`, `P(1)=2`, and `P(3)=2`. After swapping coordinates, input 2 would have three outputs. [2]  
**Thinking move:** To decide whether an inverse is a function, test horizontal lines on the original graph; a repeated output becomes a repeated input in the inverse.

**3. Transforming a function [5]**  
a. Match the original input 4: `-2(x-1)=4`, hence `x=-1`. The new output is `3(2)-1=5`, so the point is `(-1,5)`. [2]  
b. The square-root input must satisfy `-2(x-1)>=0`, so domain `(-infinity,1]`. Since `f` is nonnegative, `g>=-1`; range `[-1,infinity)`. [2]  
c. The horizontal transformation reflects the graph in the y-axis, compresses it by factor `1/2`, and shifts it right 1. [1]  
**Thinking move:** For a point on a transformed graph, set the whole inside expression equal to the old x-coordinate; do not multiply the old x-coordinate by the inside coefficient.

**4. Inverse relations [5]**  
a. Original `q` has domain all reals and range `[-1,infinity)`. The inverse relation therefore has domain `[-1,infinity)` and range `(-infinity,infinity)`. [2]  
b. The parabola fails the horizontal-line test: for example `q(-1)=0=q(-3)`, so the inverse would send 0 to two outputs. [1]  
c. One valid restriction is `x>=-2`; swap x and y in `y=(x+2)^2-1`, then take the positive root to obtain `q^(-1)(x)=-2+sqrt(x+1)`, domain `x>=-1`. Accept the alternative restriction `x<=-2` and inverse `-2-sqrt(x+1)`. [2]  
**Thinking move:** Restrict to one side of the vertex before solving the inverse. The restriction decides which square-root branch is valid.

**5. Observation wheel diagram [7]**  
a. Centre height `2+4=6 m`; greatest seat height `6+4=10 m`. [2]  
b. Domain `[0,600]` seconds; range `[2,10]` metres. [2]  
c. Twelve metres adds the radius to the already maximum height, or treats the centre as 8 m. The centre is 6 m, so the top is 10 m. [1]  
d. Draw one smooth cycle starting at `(0,2)`, rising to `(300,10)`, and falling to `(600,2)`. Label the axes time (s) and height (m). [2]  
**Thinking move:** Read the bottom clearance and radius separately. Minimum = clearance; centre = minimum + radius; maximum = centre + radius. Half a rotation takes half the time.

**6. Average and instantaneous rates [8]**  
a. `H(0)=1` and `H(2)=33`; average rate `(33-1)/(2-0)=16 m/s`. [2]  
b. `H(1.9)=32.16` and `H(2.1)=33.76`; centred secant estimate `(33.76-32.16)/(2.1-1.9)=8 m/s`. [3]  
c. Complete the square: `H(t)=-4(t-3)^2+37`. The maximum is `37 m` at `t=3 s`. [2]  
d. The first rate averages motion over two seconds, during which the ball's upward speed changes. The second estimates the rate only near `t=2`; the two intervals describe different aspects of the same motion. [1]  
**Thinking move:** A secant slope uses two separated times. A tangent estimate uses times very close to the instant of interest. Always attach height-per-time units.

**8. Secant and tangent diagram [5]**  
a. Line `AB` is the secant and represents the average rate: `(5-1)/(3-1)=2` units of y per unit of x. [2]  
b. The horizontal line through `A` is tangent at `A` and represents the instantaneous rate there; slope `0`. [2]  
c. The secant measures the whole interval from 1 to 3, where the curve rises. The tangent describes the curve's direction only at the minimum `A`, where it is momentarily flat. [1]  
**Thinking move:** Identify the interval first: average rate is a secant. Identify the single x-value next: instantaneous rate is a tangent.
